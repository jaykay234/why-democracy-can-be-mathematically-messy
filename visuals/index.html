Why Democracy Can Be Mathematically Messy

A fun but serious look at how First-Past-The-Post can turn a three-force election into a hung Assembly, and how a different aggregation rule can tell a different story from the same votes. Inspired by Veritasium's Why Democracy Is Mathematically Impossible.

Snapshot Majority: 118

Inspired by voting theory

The problem is not voters. It is the rulebook.

With two serious choices, a majority can feel obvious. With three or more serious choices, the meaning of "the winner" starts depending heavily on the counting rule. This is the family of problems explored by voting theory, including Arrow's impossibility theorem.

Historical context since 1967

Tamil Nadu was usually a two-pole contest.

Since the Dravidian-era rupture in 1967, Assembly elections were usually organized around two large poles: first Congress versus DMK, then DMK-led versus AIADMK-led alliances. The 2026 result is interesting because TVK makes the old two-pole story harder to use.

The serious point

The voters did one thing. The rulebook decided what that thing meant.

Arrow's theorem does not prove this election was wrong, and this district model is not a constitutional proposal. The point is narrower and more useful: once there are three or more serious choices, collective choice can become unstable, strategic, and rule-dependent.
